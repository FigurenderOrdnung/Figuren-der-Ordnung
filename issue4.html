FIGURES OF ORDER IV

Suspension

DE EN

The present issue of Figures of Order is entitled Suspension. At its centre is the question of how, under conditions of permanent acceleration, media-driven agitation, technological logics of execution, and political pressure to act, spaces can be reclaimed in which perception, judgement, and responsible action become possible. Suspension does not mean postponement, inactivity, or evasion. It designates the temporal form of the in-between: the interval in which an event can become legible, a situation open to examination, and a decision capable of being responsibly made.

From metaxy and Benjamin’s emergency brake through institutional forms of suspension and the occupation of the in-between to Kafka’s intermediate creatures, Alexandra Christou’s damaged spaces, and the political force of weakness, the issue examines forms of pausing, interruption, and keeping-open. What they share is the question of how spaces in between can be defended against affect, apparatus, and the dictate of immediacy.

Four concepts form a loose but distinct constellation: metaxy designates the field of tension in which opposites can remain in relation; suspension its possible temporal form. Interruption or the halt designates the operation by which such time can be gained in the first place; space for action the political or institutional form that may emerge from it. The terms are not interchangeable – and the contributions to this issue also explore the transitions and tensions between them.

1. Metaxy

Towards a Theory of Political Space for Action

Hans Holl

Metaxy designates the in-between not as a middle ground or compromise, but as a field of tension in which opposites can be held in relation. Moving from Plato and Simone Weil to the political present, the essay asks what happens when such spaces are increasingly occupied by affect and apparatus. Political order can then also be judged by whether it preserves the spaces and times in which perception, judgement, and responsible action remain possible.

2. Emergency Brake

Bringing History to a Halt: On Interruption, Catastrophe, and the Political Present

Elena Voss

Taking Walter Benjamin’s image of the emergency brake as its point of departure, the essay asks about the political meaning of bringing processes to a halt. Under conditions of accelerated decision-making, interruption can itself become a political form: not as a solution, but as the recovery of the time in which signs can be examined, situations made legible, and decisions made responsibly. The halt does not save. It wins back time.

3. Suspension

Democracy as an Order of Practised Suspension

Jürgen Miller

Suspension appears here as a political capacity and an institutional form. Through historical and contemporary examples, the essay examines procedures that insert time between event and decision: hearings, second readings, vetoes, moratoria, mediation, and constitutional review. Democracy, in this sense, is also an order of suspension – an order that keeps open the time in which judgement can emerge.

GUEST CONTRIBUTION

4. War and Individual Rights

Philipp Ammon

War does not only raise questions of state sovereignty and international order. It also transforms the relation between individual rights and the claims of the state. Philipp Ammon examines how geography, historical experience, and security shape different traditions of statehood, and how permanent confrontation erodes the conditions under which rights and the rule of law can flourish. Détente, in this perspective, becomes more than foreign policy: it is a condition for preserving the intermediate spaces in which law can remain effective.

5. The Occupation of the In-Between

Dorothee Saffer

Political order depends on an in-between: spaces of mediation, translation, and negotiation in which conflicts can become visible without having to be decided immediately. The essay examines how these spaces are increasingly occupied. Where the public sphere itself becomes a site of permanent mobilisation, the in-between changes from a space of political mediation into a contested landscape.

6. Predators and Apparatuses

Elena Voss

Two seemingly opposed forms of power converge in the destruction of the in-between. Apparatuses translate political situations into procedures, administrative responsibilities, and technical execution; predators rely on surprise, acceleration, and direct seizure. Algorithmic governance and political disruption thus appear not as opposites, but as different forms of an order in which mediation, hesitation, and institutional resistance increasingly disappear.

7. Kafka’s Intermediate Creatures

Georg Magoni

Kafka’s texts are populated by creatures that resist unambiguous classification: animal and human, thing and living being, servant and master, accused and free citizen. Unlike the hybrid figures of myth, these intermediate creatures have no fixed place within an order. They inhabit doors, corridors, offices, stages, and bodies – damaged spaces in between in which a decision remains pending, perhaps one that can no longer be made.

8. Alexandra Christou’s Spaces

Hans Holl

Alexandra Christou (1950–2009) was a Greek self-taught painter whose work received wider attention only posthumously. Her paintings often depict markets, tavernas, and other social spaces of everyday Greek life, while also exploring female experience in public and private space.

Christou’s paintings construct spaces that are neither scenery nor mere backgrounds for their figures. Surfaces, thresholds, bridges, walls, and textures become elements of a damaged world in which people appear less as autonomous figures than as nodes within precarious relations. The essay asks how Christou creates from these fractures distinctive forms of social space – spaces that keep their damage visible rather than reconciling it.

9. The Power of Weakness

The Political Force of Illegibility

Miriam Klose

Weakness is not simply the opposite of power. Drawing on Michel de Certeau and James C. Scott, the essay examines practices that emerge precisely where a place of one’s own and institutionally protected spaces for action are absent. Tactics, withdrawal, illegibility, and local knowledge limit the translation of power into execution. The strength of weakness lies in turning the absence of a place of one’s own into room for manoeuvre.

OBJECTION

Who Can Afford Suspension?

Miriam Klose

Suspension, and the time it creates, are not equally available to everyone. Those with resources, institutional protection, money, status, or secure immigration status are better able to wait than those facing deportation, war, poverty, precarious employment, or immediate violence. Any theory of suspension must therefore also take account of the unequal distribution of time.

HOW TO DIE?

Between Death and Disappearance

What happens in the interval between dying and disappearance? The Buddhist figure of antarābhava describes an intermediate state in which the stream of existence continues after death without yet having assumed a new form. Simone Weil’s décréation takes the question in another direction: not the persistence of the self, but its withdrawal. Two very different figures of thought meet in the idea of a transition that is neither mere ending nor already a new beginning.

NOTE

A House for Giuseppe Panza

Robert Wilson · Villa Panza

A house within a house: Robert Wilson’s A House for Giuseppe Panza stands in the park of Villa Panza in Varese. Its interior can be seen through the windows but remains closed to visitors. A chair, a table, an open book, and a hand suspended above it form a tableau held outside ordinary time. Reading, study, contemplation, and silence become the inhabitants of a space that can be seen but not entered.
